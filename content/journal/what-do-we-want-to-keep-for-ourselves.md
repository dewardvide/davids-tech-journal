AI is becoming very good at helping us make decisions.

That sounds obvious when we talk about writing emails, summarizing documents, generating code, planning trips, or organizing a calendar. But I think the more interesting shift is happening somewhere else. AI is becoming useful not just for doing things, but for thinking through things.

You can give it a problem and ask it to structure your thoughts. You can explain what you're planning and ask it to challenge your assumptions. You can ask it to compare options, point out risks, optimize a plan, or suggest an entirely different approach. And often, the answer is good enough to change your mind.

That's where the relationship gets interesting.

The question isn't whether AI is going to "take control" of our lives in some dramatic or dystopian way. I don't think that's a particularly useful framing. In most cases, nobody is forcing us to use these systems. We invite them in because they're useful.

The more interesting question is what happens when they become useful enough that we start handing over parts of our lives almost without noticing.

## Influence doesn't require control

Imagine I've already made a decision about something. I explain my reasoning to an AI and ask for a second opinion. It gives me a better argument for another option, points out something I missed, and I change my mind. I still made the decision, nothing was forced on me. But it'd be hard to say the AI had no influence over the outcome.

That in itself isn't necessarily concerning. Human beings have always influenced one another. Friends, books, teachers, colleagues, journalists, and the cultures we grow up in all shape the way we think. There's nothing uniquely sinister about an external source changing our mind. What feels different about AI is the potential scale and intimacy of that influence.

An AI system can increasingly understand the context of a problem, remember your preferences, account for your constraints, compare far more information than you could reasonably process yourself, and present an argument tailored specifically to you. As these systems improve, their advice may become genuinely hard to dismiss.

At some point, ignoring the recommendation might even feel irrational.

And perhaps that's fine?

## There are plenty of things I'm happy to delegate

There are plenty of areas of life where I'd gladly let AI do more.

Work is an obvious example. If AI can help me research faster, automate repetitive tasks, analyze data, review code, summarize meetings, or handle admin work, I see little reason not to use it.

The same applies to a lot of everyday life that's largely about optimization. I'd happily let AI help me structure my budget, compare insurance policies, plan a trip, manage paperwork, organize a schedule, or find a more efficient way to do something I already have to do. These are useful tasks, but I don't derive much meaning from doing them manually. If AI can save me time there, that's a clear benefit. But once you follow that logic far enough, another question appears.

## What shouldn't I optimize away?

There are activities where the end result is only part of the value.

Writing is an obvious example. A perfectly written paragraph may not be as meaningful to me if I didn't struggle to find the words myself. The same might be true of learning something difficult, making something by hand, playing an instrument, cooking for someone, choosing a gift, or working through a problem that initially seemed beyond you. An AI may eventually produce a better result in all of these areas. But at times producing the best result isn't the point.

Sometimes the value is in the process. Sometimes it's in the effort. Sometimes it's in the fact that the outcome represents something you learned, attempted, understood, or created yourself.

That's where the conversation around AI gets personal.

The important question isn't just "What can AI do for me?" We already know that answer is expanding fast. A more interesting question is: "What do I want to keep for myself?"

## Everyone will probably draw that boundary differently

I don't think there's one correct answer.

For one person, writing may be something they never want to delegate, because writing is how they think. Someone else may have no attachment to writing at all and would happily let an AI handle most of it. One person may use AI extensively at work but want to keep it completely out of their relationships. Another may use it to think through relationship problems but never want it involved in creative hobbies.

The boundary will be different for everyone because the things we find meaningful are different. That's why the more important thing isn't where you draw the boundary - it's whether you've thought about it at all.

## The risk is that we won't notice what we've given up

This is probably the part I find most interesting.

AI doesn't need to become malicious for us to lose something. It just needs to become convenient enough. If an AI writes better than you, plans better than you, remembers more than you, compares options more effectively than you, and gives consistently useful advice, the natural thing to do is keep using it. Over time, you may stop doing certain things yourself.

Maybe you stop writing from scratch because AI can get you 80 percent of the way there immediately. Maybe you stop navigating unfamiliar places because an assistant always tells you where to go. Maybe you stop wrestling with difficult decisions because AI can lay out the options more clearly. Maybe you stop developing certain skills because there's no longer much practical reason to.

None of these choices are automatically wrong. What matters is whether they're choices at all.

There's a difference between deciding something's no longer worth doing yourself, and realizing years later that you gradually stopped doing it because outsourcing became easier. That difference seems important.

## Maybe the question matters more than the answer

I don't currently have a clean list of what I would and wouldn't delegate to AI. I'm not even sure such a list would stay useful for long, given how fast these systems are changing.

But I do think it's worth asking the question now.

> What parts of my life do I want AI to improve? What parts am I comfortable outsourcing almost completely? And which parts would feel diminished if I stopped doing them myself?

The point isn't to draw an arbitrary line around "human" activities and defend them from technology. It's simply to notice that efficiency and value aren't always the same thing.

Some things are valuable precisely because they take time. Some things matter because they require effort. Some things may even be worth doing badly, simply because doing them is part of who we are.

As AI becomes capable of doing more and more for us, deciding what to delegate will keep getting easier. The harder question is what we still want to keep for ourselves.
